How a flat photo becomes 3D

A single photograph has no depth information. It is a grid of colors. Yet you can look at one and instantly tell that the face is in front of the wall, that the road recedes toward the hills. A neural network can learn that same intuition from millions of examples, and the surprising part is that it now fits in a browser tab.

  • The model. DepthScope runs Depth Anything V2, a monocular depth estimator. It takes your photo and outputs a second grayscale image: a depth map, where bright means close and dark means far.
  • Where it runs. On your own GPU, through WebGPU. The first photo is slower because the model file downloads once and is then cached. After that, estimation takes a second or two. Your photo is never sent anywhere, because there is no server doing the work. There is no server at all.
  • The 3D. DepthScope builds a fine mesh, paints your photo onto it as a texture, and pushes each point toward or away from you according to the depth map. The flat picture becomes a relief surface you can orbit, light, and record.

Getting the best result

Photos with a clear foreground subject and a receding background pop the most: portraits, a person on a street, a path into a landscape. Flat, head-on shots (a document, a wall) have little real depth, so there is little for the model to find. Use the depth-pop slider to exaggerate or calm the effect.

Honest limits

Monocular depth is an educated guess, not a measurement. The model infers relative depth, not true distance, and it can be fooled by reflections, transparency, and unusual scenes. WebGPU is required for the fast path; browsers without it (some older mobile, Linux Firefox) fall back to a slower CPU mode or are unsupported. This is a toy that happens to use a serious model, not a metrology tool.
